Verb Master Map.

A verb is not only an action word. In English, the verb is the engine of the sentence: it carries action, state, time, aspect, voice, mood, questions, negation, and meaning. Master the verb, and the sentence starts obeying you.

01 · The verb engine

Stop memorizing verbs as isolated words.

Weak method: “eat = comer.” Strong method: verb + subject + time + structure + context. A verb becomes useful only when you can move it through sentences.

The complete verb equation: meaning + structure + time.

Every English verb answers three control questions: What kind of verb is it? What time/aspect does it use? What role does it play in the sentence?

Master rule: English questions and negatives usually need an auxiliary. If the student does not control auxiliaries, verbs will feel chaotic.
02 · Verb types

Know what kind of verb you are using.

The same word can behave differently depending on context. “Look” can be an action verb, a linking verb, or part of a phrasal verb.

Action verbs

They show movement or activity.

They can be physical or mental. They usually answer: “What does the subject do?”

I run every morning.
She writes emails.
They think deeply.
Linking verbs

They connect subject and description.

They do not show action. They link the subject to a state, identity, or quality.

He is tired.
The soup tastes good.
She seems nervous.
Auxiliary verbs

They build grammar.

Auxiliaries support tense, questions, negatives, perfect forms, progressive forms, and passive voice.

Do you work?
She is studying.
They have finished.
Modal verbs

They add attitude and possibility.

Modals do not take -s and are followed by the base verb.

He can swim.
You should rest.
They must leave.
Stative verbs

They describe states, not actions.

Many stative verbs resist progressive form unless the meaning changes.

I know the answer.
She owns a car.
They believe him.
Phrasal verbs

Verb + particle = new meaning.

The particle can change the meaning completely. “Look” is not the same as “look up.”

Look up the word.
Turn off the light.
Give up smoking.
03 · Tense and aspect

Time is not enough. Aspect explains the shape of the action.

Tense tells when. Aspect tells how the speaker sees the action: complete, repeated, in progress, or connected to another time.

Verb Sentence Builder.

Choose a subject, verb, tense, and function. The sentence will change. This trains the hidden machinery: auxiliary, main verb, and complement.

I work every day.

Present simple uses the base verb with I/you/we/they.

05 · Voice and mood

Who does the action? What is the speaker's intention?

Voice changes the focus. Mood changes the intention. These two controls make English flexible and mature.

Active vs Passive Voice

Active: The subject does the action.
Passive: The subject receives the action.

Active: The chef cooks dinner.
Passive: Dinner is cooked by the chef.
Formula: object + be + past participle + by + agent.

Mood: intention of the speaker

Mood is not emotion. It is the grammatical intention behind the verb.

Indicative: He goes to class.
Imperative: Go to class.
Subjunctive: I wish he were here.
High-level note: Subjunctive is limited but important in formal English, wishes, and hypotheticals.
06 · Verb patterns

Some verbs demand a specific next form.

This is where many intermediate students get trapped. You must learn verbs together with the structure they control.

Verb + to

want / need / decide / hope

These verbs usually take an infinitive after them.

I want to improve.
She decided to leave.
Verb + ing

enjoy / avoid / finish / keep

These verbs usually take a gerund after them.

I enjoy learning.
He avoids speaking.
Verb + object + to

tell / ask / invite / allow

The object receives the instruction or invitation.

She told me to wait.
They invited us to join.
Modal + base

can / must / should / might

Modal verbs never need “to” before the next verb.

You should study.
He can drive.
Causative verbs

make / let / have

These verbs can force a base verb pattern.

My teacher made me repeat.
Let me try.
Linking + adjective

be / seem / feel / look

After linking verbs, describe the subject with an adjective.

She looks happy.
The soup tastes good.
07 · Same idea, stronger verbs

Watch one idea grow from A1 to C2.

This makes progress visible. Higher levels do not only use “harder words”; they control time, nuance, causality, voice, and stance.

A1

I work every day.

A2

I usually work in the morning, but today I am resting.

B1

I have worked hard this year because I want to improve my opportunities.

B2

I had been working without a clear method until I realized that consistency mattered more than intensity.

C1

My progress accelerated once my practice was structured around feedback, retrieval, and deliberate repetition.

C2

What had once felt like mechanical study gradually evolved into an automatic command of patterns, nuance, and communicative intention.

08 · Core verb vocabulary

Train high-frequency verbs first.

Do not waste early energy on rare verbs. Build command over verbs that create thousands of sentences.

09 · Mastery check

Prove that the map is working.

Answer by structure, not by instinct. The goal is not luck. The goal is controlled explanation.

Teacher route

How to teach this live.

  1. Open with the engine idea: a verb is not a translation; it is the sentence motor.
  2. Use three example verbs: work, be, can. Show action, linking, modal.
  3. Move one verb through forms: work, works, worked, working, have worked, is worked.
  4. Contrast time and aspect: I work / I am working / I have worked / I had been working.
  5. Force auxiliary control: positive → negative → question.
  6. End with production: students create one sentence at A1, A2, B1, B2, C1.
