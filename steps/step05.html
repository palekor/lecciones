01 · The learning model

Space is either a point, a surface, or a container.

Spanish speakers usually reach for "en" and translate it as "in." English forces a choice first: is this location a precise point, a flat surface, or an enclosed space? That single decision predicts the correct preposition almost every time.

Three questions replace one habit. Ask before you speak.

Before choosing a preposition of place, picture the location. Is it a dot on a map, a flat top you could set a cup on, or a box you could close the lid on? That mental image — not translation — gives you the answer.

AT · POINTAn exact spot

A precise address, an event, or a specific location treated as a single point: at the door, at the party, at 221B Baker Street.

ON · SURFACEA flat contact

Something touching a surface, line, or edge: a table, a wall, a floor, a street, a page: on the desk, on Fifth Avenue, on page 12.

IN · CONTAINERAn enclosed space

Something bounded on multiple sides: a box, a room, a city, a book, a year: in the drawer, in Bogotá, in 2026.

Important: when a sentence feels wrong, don't ask "how do I say en in English?" Ask "is this a point, a surface, or a container?" The hesitation is the grammar working, not a mistake.

Position vs. movement

Everything in this room describes a fixed position. Later, in the atlas, you'll meet the movement group: into, onto, out of, through, across — the same logic, but with a verb of motion.

Why pairs matter

English rarely gives you one word per relationship. Under/below, over/above, between/among, next to/near each split one Spanish idea into two English choices with a real difference in meaning.

Use the room as a check

If you can picture where the cat, the box, the lamp, or the rug is, you can usually hear which preposition is wrong before you can explain the rule.

03 · The other half of the sentence

A preposition needs an article to finish the picture.

"In box" and "in a box" and "in the box" are three different sentences. The preposition tells you the spatial logic; the article tells you whether the listener already knows which box.

Definite

the

  • The listener can identify exactly which one.
  • Already mentioned, unique, or made specific by context.
  • Superlatives and one-of-a-kind places.
"The keys are on the table." (there's one specific table we both know.)
Indefinite

a / an

  • One member of a group; the listener doesn't know which one yet.
  • First mention of a countable, singular noun.
  • "An" before a vowel sound, not just a vowel letter.
"There's a box under the table." (any box — new information.)
Zero article

Ø

  • Plural or uncountable nouns used in a general sense.
  • Most cities, countries, and streets: in Bogotá, on Elm Street.
  • Fixed expressions about purpose, not the physical place.
"She's Ø at school." (she's studying — not describing the building.)
The trap for Spanish speakers: a handful of place nouns drop the article when you mean their normal purpose, and take "the" only when you mean the physical building or object.
Ø · purpose
She's in bed. (resting)
He's at school. (studying)
They're in prison. (serving a sentence)
the · physical place
The cat is on the bed. (the furniture)
I left my bag at the school. (the building)
They painted the prison. (the structure)
